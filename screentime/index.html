Screentime

What the research is actually saying

Dr. Jason Horne  ·  Greeneville City Schools
The noise

Everyone has an opinion about kids and screens.

Most of those opinions come from headlines, not evidence. So let's start by setting the headlines aside.

"Smartphones are rewiring a generation" "Ban the phones, save the kids" "Screens are destroying attention spans" "Tech in schools is a failed experiment" "Just give every student a laptop"
Why now?

Why this is suddenly everywhere.

01

A policy wave

By the end of 2025, 35 states plus D.C. had restricted phones in schools, 22 of them in 2025 alone.

02

A health alarm

The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory named social media a risk to youth mental health.

03

A best-selling backlash

Horvath's The Digital Delusion and his 2026 Senate testimony pushed "screens hurt learning" into the mainstream.

Ballotpedia (2025); U.S. Surgeon General (2023); Horvath, U.S. Senate testimony (January 2026).
The finding

It is the type of use, not the total time, that decides whether screens help or harm learning.

Across 51 findings from 45 sources, aggregate screen time is a poor predictor. What the screen is doing matters far more than how long it is on.

Synthesis of 45 sources, government, academic, and news. UNESCO (2023); OECD (2024); JAMA Pediatrics meta-analysis (2019).
When screens hurt

Passive and recreational use drags learning down.

01

Television

Inversely linked to both language and math scores. More viewing, lower performance.

02

Video gaming

Inversely associated with composite academic scores across studies.

03

The dosage effect

Adverse effects become pronounced once passive use passes roughly three hours a day.

Adelantado-Renau et al. (2019), JAMA Pediatrics meta-analysis; Li et al. (2025), Frontiers in Public Health.
The threshold
3hrs

is the daily line where risk to mental health and academics accelerates.

Teens spending more than three hours a day on social media face roughly double the risk of depression and anxiety.

That risk is a primary driver of declining academic engagement.

U.S. Surgeon General (2023), Social Media and Youth Mental Health Advisory; CDC (2025).
The cost of distraction
49 pts

The math-score gap between students with under one hour and those with five to seven hours of leisure screen time at school. Roughly more than a full year of schooling.

OECD (2024), Students, Digital Devices and Success, drawing on PISA 2022. Bars shown proportionally.
When screens help

Purposeful, teacher-guided use can lift outcomes.

  • Moderate device use for learning, about one to five hours, beats non-use on PISA math.
  • For ages one to eight, story-congruent interactive features can aid comprehension.
  • The quality of the interaction matters more than the quantity of time.
OECD (2024), Technology Use at School and Students' Learning Outcomes; Furenes et al. (2021).
The EdTech paradox
<2%

of education-technology interventions have strong or moderate evidence of effectiveness.

UNESCO 2023 Global Education Monitoring Report.

Hardware without pedagogy is not a strategy. Professional development is the variable that decides whether devices work.

UNESCO (2023), Global Education Monitoring Report: Technology in Education.
The accountability turn

Some districts now pay vendors only if students actually improve.

3 of 4
Rigorously evaluated interventions under outcomes-based contracts showed positive academic gains.
20+
States experimenting with the approach. Michigan put $50 million into virtual tutoring this way.
$0
Documented savings. Spending looked about the same as under traditional contracts.

For literacy specifically: the AI reading program lifted second-grade proficiency and did nothing for older students. Match the tool to the grade band. Each district also received roughly $80,000 in coaching, so the support may matter as much as the contract language.

Barshay (2026), The Hechinger Report, on WestEd's study of 8 early-adopter districts in CA, FL, MS, and TX, August 2024 to March 2026.
A voice fueling the debate
"Most general-use educational technologies perform below the effectiveness of ordinary classroom instruction."
Dr. Jared Cooney Horvath, cognitive neuroscientist. MEd, Mind, Brain & Education, Harvard; PhD, University of Melbourne. U.S. Senate Commerce Committee testimony, January 15, 2026.

The C-SPAN clip passed two million views. This is the moment the screen debate stopped being a parent-group conversation and became federal policy.

Horvath, U.S. Senate written testimony (Jan. 15, 2026); Horvath, The Digital Delusion (Harmony/Penguin Random House, 2026).
Read the fine print

That "below ordinary instruction" number rests on one choice.

What he did

He subtracted 0.42 from every published effect size, on the theory that 0.42 is what ordinary classroom teaching is worth. His own note: Adjusted Effect = Reported d − 0.42. That single subtraction is what turns small positives into negatives.

What statisticians say

Elizabeth Tipton of Northwestern calls the benchmark "wildly unrealistic," noting that in a carefully designed study "an effect above zero would be encouraging." The disagreement is about the yardstick, not the data.

Take the distraction argument seriously. Even his critics concede it. Just know that the headline number is a modeling choice you are allowed to question.

Horvath, U.S. Senate written testimony (2026), pp. 4-5; Barnum (2026), Chalkbeat.
Policy that outran the evidence

Phone bans went national before anyone tested them.

6.4%
Of a standard deviation: the 2016 English gain that launched the movement. Low achievers gained 14.2%.
~30
States that had already legislated before the first causal study was published in October 2025.
~0
Average effect on test scores in the largest, pre-registered national study, April 2026.

The pattern across twenty years: the better the study design, the smaller the effect. Bans are defensible on attention and behavior. They were oversold as an achievement fix.

Beland & Murphy (2016), Labour Economics; Figlio & Ozek (2025), NBER w34388 (Florida); Allcott, Baron, Dee, Duckworth, Gentzkow & Jacob (2026), NBER w35132.
But context matters

Two cautions before you copy the policy.

The Sweden contradiction

The same design run on every Swedish secondary school found no effect, and could rule out even small gains. Students already had laptops and tablets everywhere, so removing phones did not remove digital distraction. The device that matters is the one in front of them.

The equity caveat

Florida saw a first-year spike in discipline, including a 30% increase in suspensions for Black students. Track discipline data by subgroup so enforcement does not widen gaps.

Kessel, Hardardottir & Tyrefors (2020), Economics of Education Review (Sweden); Barshay (2025), The Hechinger Report (equity).
Reading specifically

For deep reading, paper still wins.

  • Students reading on paper consistently outperform those reading the same text on screens.
  • The gap widens for informational text, and under time pressure or unsupervised conditions.
  • Scrolling on small screens reduces comprehension further still.

This is the "screen inferiority effect," and it lands squarely in our work as literacy educators.

Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus (2021), Review of Educational Research; Delgado et al. (2018), Educational Research Review; Kong, Seo & Zhai (2018).
The hidden mediator
1 in 3

adolescents use screens until midnight or later.

Screens often reach the gradebook indirectly, through lost sleep and social media stress.

The path to grades runs through attention, sleep, and mental health. Address those and the academics follow.

Pew Research Center (2024), Teens, Social Media and Technology; WHO Europe (2024).
May 20, 2026

A federal health advisory just told schools to go back to paper.

The Surgeon General's Warning on the Harms of Screen Use shipped a 29-page toolkit asking schools to "limit screen use by assigning work in books or on paper whenever possible," and to adopt bell-to-bell phone restrictions.

This is the first time federal health guidance has told schools to move student work off screens. Whatever you think of it, it lands squarely on our desks as literacy educators.

U.S. Surgeon General / HHS (May 20, 2026), Warning on the Harms of Screen Use: An Advisory and Toolkit.
Closer to home

Tennessee has already legislated this. Twice.

2025: the student's phone

Public Chapter 103, effective July 1, 2025. Every district must bar wireless communication devices during instructional time. Note the breadth: phones, smartwatches, tablets, laptops, and gaming devices.

2026: the district's Chromebook

Public Chapter 808, effective July 1, 2026. K-5 districts must adopt policies minimizing unnecessary screen time. Passed the House 87-6. The target moved from what students bring to what we hand them.

The sponsor's stated reason is our reason: "Young students develop literacy and reasoning through books, handwriting, discussion and direct instruction."

TN General Assembly, HB0932 (Public Chapter 103) and HB2393 (Public Chapter 808), verified against the TN legislature bill records.
What to do Monday

The evidence-based playbook.

01

Store phones bell to bell

Pouches or lockers for personal devices. Expect academic gains to show by year two.

02

Keep paper for deep reading

Use print for expository reading and assessment to dodge the screen inferiority effect.

03

PD before devices

Never hand out hardware without teacher training. Pedagogy is what makes 1:1 work.

04

Name the two uses

Separate recreational use (limit it) from instructional use (teacher-led) in policy.

The takeaway

Be the evidence in the building.

It is the type of use, not the time. Sort the strong findings from the shaky ones, and lead the conversation with teachers, parents, and students.
